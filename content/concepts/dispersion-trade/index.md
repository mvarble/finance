title: Dispersion trade

A dispersion trade is a financial strategy that bets that outcomes within a group of related assets will diverge — some will perform well while others perform poorly — rather than all moving in the same direction. Instead of taking a directional view on an entire sector or market, the trade takes a view on _differences within_ that sector. It profits when predictions about relative performance are correct, regardless of whether the group as a whole goes up or down.

The literal version of the trade lives in derivatives markets. Consider a trader who looks at the S&P 500 technology sector and concludes that the sector as a whole will not move much, but that individual tech stocks will swing widely in different directions — some will report blowout earnings while others will miss expectations. The trader could sell index options on the tech sector (betting that the index as a whole will remain relatively stable) while buying options on the individual stocks (betting that the individual stocks will move significantly). If the individual stocks move in offsetting directions — some rising sharply, others falling sharply — the index stays roughly flat (the short index options are safe) but the individual stock options gain value from the large moves. The trader profits from the _dispersion_ of returns across the group.

The mechanics of this literal trade involve a concept called correlation. When the stocks in an index all move together (high correlation), the index itself moves a lot, and the individual stock moves do not generate much extra value relative to the index. When the stocks move in different directions (low correlation), the index stays flat but the individual stock moves are large. A dispersion trade is, at its core, a bet that correlation will be lower than the market expects — that individual outcomes will be more spread out, more dispersed, than current pricing implies.

The term is also used more broadly as a mental model for any investment thesis that identifies winners and losers within a single theme. Rather than saying "this trend is good for the whole industry," a dispersion thesis says "this trend will benefit some participants and harm others within the same industry, and I can profit from that divergence." For example, a rise in interest rates might be good for banks that earn large [net interest margins](../net-interest-margin/) but bad for mortgage lenders whose refinancing volume dries up. An investor with this view would buy bank stocks and sell mortgage lender stocks — a dispersion trade on the theme of rising rates.

The key characteristic of any dispersion trade is that it is _market-neutral_ with respect to the overall theme. The entire sector need not go up or down for the trade to work; only the predictions about which participants win and which lose must be correct. This makes dispersion trades appealing when there are strong views about relative performance within a group but less conviction about the group's overall direction. The risk is that the winners and losers are misidentified — or that an unexpected shock causes the entire group to move together in a way that overwhelms the relative positions.
