Prediction markets are financial markets where participants trade contracts whose payoffs depend on the outcome of future events. These events can include elections, economic data releases, sporting events, geopolitical developments, or any other uncertain future occurrence. Contracts are priced according to the market's collective estimate of the probability of each outcome, and traders buy or sell based on whether they believe the market's estimate is too low or too high.

Consider a concrete example. A prediction market offers contracts on a presidential election. A contract that pays $1 if Candidate A wins is currently trading at $0.55, implying the market assigns a 55% probability to Candidate A winning. A trader who believes Candidate A is actually 70% likely to win buys the contract at $0.55 — the expected value is $0.70, yielding an expected profit of $0.15 per contract. A trader who believes Candidate A is only 40% likely to win sells (or shorts) the contract. When the election resolves, the winning contract pays $1 and the losing contract pays $0. Traders who were correct profit; traders who were wrong lose their investment.

Sportsbooks operate on a similar principle but in a more traditional format. A sportsbook sets odds or a point spread for a sporting event and accepts bets from customers. For example, in an NFL game, the sportsbook might set the Kansas City Chiefs as 7-point favorites over the Denver Broncos, with both sides priced at -110 (meaning a bettor must wager $110 to win $100). The -110 pricing on both sides means the sportsbook collects $110 from Chiefs bettors and $110 from Broncos bettors on every matched pair of bets, pays out $210 to the winning side, and keeps the remaining $10 as profit. This built-in margin is called the _vigorish_ (or "vig" or "juice"), and it ensures the sportsbook profits regardless of which side wins, as long as it balances the bets roughly equally on both sides.

Prediction markets and sportsbooks share several structural characteristics that make them highly profitable, particularly when volume is high. The first is wide margins. The bid-ask spread or vigorish on these contracts is much wider than on traditional financial instruments like stocks. A stock might have a bid-ask spread of $0.01 on a $100 share (0.01%). A sports bet at -110 has an effective margin of about 4.5%. A prediction market contract might have a spread of $0.03 to $0.05 on a $0.55 contract (5% to 9%). These wide margins exist because outcomes are highly uncertain and the platforms need a larger buffer against adverse moves.

The second characteristic is low [adverse selection](../adverse-selection/) risk. While some bettors may have genuine expertise — a professional poker player, a seasoned political analyst, or a sophisticated quantitative model — the vast majority of retail participants have no meaningful informational advantage over the market. A casual sports fan betting on the weekend's football games, or an individual trader buying election contracts based on gut feeling, is an uninformed counterparty. The house (whether a sportsbook or a prediction market platform) is therefore relatively safe on either side of the bet, and the wide margin provides additional cushion against the occasional sharp bettor.

The third characteristic is that revenue scales directly with volume. Unlike traditional investments — where a customer who buys a stock and holds it for years generates little ongoing revenue — every bet or prediction market trade generates margin for the platform. A customer who makes 100 bets per week generates 100 times the margin of a customer who makes one bet. More activity directly equals more revenue, regardless of whether the customer wins or loses.

These three characteristics — wide margins, low adverse selection risk, and volume-driven revenue — make prediction markets and sportsbooks especially profitable when trading volume is high. Any force that increases the frequency of bets and trades without meaningfully improving the bettor's odds of winning is highly beneficial to these platforms. A customer who bets occasionally based on personal interest generates modest revenue. A customer whose automated systems place bets continuously — researching, analyzing, and trading around the clock — generates many times more revenue, because each trade contributes margin to the platform. The customer's increased activity does not improve their expected returns (the house edge remains the same), but it multiplies the platform's revenue.
