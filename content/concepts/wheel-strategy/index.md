The wheel strategy is a popular options trading strategy designed to generate regular income from option premiums. It is a mechanical, rules-based approach that cycles between selling put options and selling call options on a stock, making it straightforward to systematize and automate. The strategy is popular among retail investors who want to earn steady income from options without taking large directional bets on stock prices.

The strategy operates in two steps that repeat in a cycle.

**Step 1: Sell a cash-secured put.** The trader chooses a stock they would be willing to own and sells (writes) a put option on it. A put option gives the buyer the right to sell the stock to the writer at a specified price (the strike price) by a specified date (the expiration date). In exchange for taking on this obligation, the writer receives a premium — cash paid upfront by the option buyer. To sell a _cash-secured_ put, the trader sets aside enough cash in their account to cover the cost of buying the stock if the option is exercised.

A concrete example makes this tangible. Suppose shares of Acme Corp are trading at $100. The trader sells one put option (covering 100 shares) with a strike price of $95 and an expiration date one month away. The buyer pays a premium of $2.00 per share, or $200 total. The trader sets aside $9,500 in cash to cover a potential purchase. Two outcomes are possible. If Acme stays above $95 through expiration, the put expires worthless: the trader keeps the $200 premium, the $9,500 is released, and Step 1 repeats. If Acme falls to $93 at expiration, the put is exercised: the trader is obligated to buy 100 shares at $95 each, spending $9,500, and now owns stock worth $9,300 (an unrealized loss of $200 on the shares, partially offset by the $200 premium collected). The trader moves to Step 2.

**Step 2: Sell a covered call.** Now in possession of the stock, the trader sells (writes) a call option on it. A call option gives the buyer the right to buy the stock from the writer at a specified strike price by a specified date. In exchange, the writer receives another premium. Because the trader actually owns the shares, this is a _covered_ call — the shares can be delivered if the option is exercised.

Continuing the example, the trader holds Acme at a cost basis of $95 and the stock is now trading at $93. The trader sells one call option (covering the 100 shares) with a strike price of $97 and an expiration one month away. The buyer pays a premium of $1.50 per share, or $150. Again, two outcomes are possible. If Acme stays below $97 through expiration, the call expires worthless: the trader keeps the $150 premium, still owns the stock, and repeats Step 2. If Acme rises to $99 at expiration, the call is exercised: the trader sells the 100 shares at $97, receiving $9,700, keeps the $150 premium plus the $200 gain on the shares ($97 sale minus $95 purchase), no longer owns the stock, and returns to Step 1.

The wheel generates income from option premiums on a regular basis — typically weekly or monthly, depending on the expiration dates chosen. In the example above, the first cycle generated $200 in premium, and the second cycle generated $150 in premium plus a $200 capital gain, for a total of $550 over two months (not accounting for the brief period of unrealized loss while holding the stock).

The main risk of the wheel strategy is a significant decline in the stock price. If Acme falls from $100 to $70 while the trader is holding it, the $25-per-share loss on the stock position ($2,500 on 100 shares) far exceeds the few hundred dollars of premium collected. The strategy works best on stable or slowly rising stocks where large sudden declines are unlikely. It also underperforms in strong bull markets: if Acme rises from $100 to $150, the trader would have been better off simply holding the stock, because the covered call capped upside at the strike price.

The wheel strategy is frequently discussed in the context of automated or systematic trading because it is entirely rules-based. The decisions — which strike price to choose, which expiration date, when to sell the next option — follow a mechanical process that can be codified and executed without human judgment on each trade. This makes it well-suited for automation: a system can monitor the stock price, select appropriate strikes and expirations based on volatility and other inputs, execute the trades, and manage the cycle continuously. For [market makers](../market-making/) and brokerages, automated wheel strategies are attractive because they generate consistent options trading volume — and options trades, with their wider spreads, produce higher [payment for order flow](../payment-for-order-flow/) revenue per trade than simple equity trades.
