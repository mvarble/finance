Net interest margin (NIM) is a measure of the difference between the interest income a bank earns on its assets and the interest it pays on its liabilities, expressed as a percentage of the bank's average earning assets. It is one of the most important indicators of a bank's profitability and is reported quarterly in earnings statements. A rising NIM generally signals expanding profits; a falling NIM signals margin compression.

The core mechanism behind NIM is the deposit spread. When a customer deposits money in a checking or savings account, the bank does not hold that cash idle. It lends the money out — as mortgages, auto loans, commercial loans, or credit card receivables — or invests it in securities such as government bonds. The bank earns a market rate of interest on these assets. Meanwhile, it pays the depositor a below-market interest rate on their deposit. The difference between these two rates is the deposit spread, and it is one of the largest sources of profit for traditional banks.

A concrete example makes the scale clear. Suppose a bank has $10 billion in deposits. It pays an average interest rate of 1% on those deposits, costing $100 million per year. It lends out or invests that $10 billion at an average rate of 5%, earning $500 million per year. The deposit spread is 4 percentage points (or 400 [basis points](../basis-points/)), and the bank earns $400 million per year from it. The bank's net interest margin — net interest income divided by average earning assets — would be approximately 4% in this simplified case. In practice, banks also have other interest-bearing liabilities (wholesale borrowing, bonds) and other earning assets (securities portfolios), so the actual NIM calculation is more involved, but the deposit spread is the conceptual heart of it.

The deposit spread depends critically on customer inertia. Most retail customers do not actively optimize their deposit accounts. They leave money in low-yield checking accounts, do not shop around for the best savings rate, and do not move money to higher-yielding alternatives when market rates rise. This inattention allows banks to keep deposit rates well below the rates they earn on their lending and investment activities. When a bank's savings account pays 0.5% and the federal funds rate is 5%, the bank is earning a massive spread on every dollar of deposits — but only because most customers do not bother to move their money elsewhere.

When competition for deposits intensifies, the spread compresses. This can happen through several channels: online banks offering higher rates (because they have lower overhead costs and need to attract deposits to fund their lending), money market funds offering yields closer to market rates, or rate-comparison tools making it easier for customers to find better options. Historically, deposit betas — the fraction of a rate increase that banks pass through to depositors — have been low. When the Federal Reserve raises rates by 100 basis points, banks might raise deposit rates by only 20 to 40 basis points, capturing the remaining 60 to 80 basis points as expanded margin. Over time, as customers slowly move their money to higher-yielding alternatives, the deposit beta rises and the margin compresses.

The same deposit-spread mechanism operates in [brokerage cash balance revenue](../brokerage-cash-balance-revenue/), where brokerages earn interest on customers' uninvested cash and pay the customer a below-market rate. The fundamental business model is identical: profit from the gap between what the institution earns on someone else's idle money and what it pays them for it.
