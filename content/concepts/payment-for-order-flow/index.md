Payment for order flow (PFOF) is the practice by which a retail brokerage receives compensation from a [market maker](../market-making/) in exchange for routing its customers' trades to that market maker for execution. It is the primary way that commission-free brokerages such as Robinhood, Schwab, and E*TRADE generate revenue from trades. This document builds on [market making](../market-making/) and [adverse selection](../adverse-selection/): the first explains how the firm that receives the orders earns its living, and the second why retail orders in particular are worth paying for.

The mechanism works as follows. When a retail customer places a trade — say, buying 100 shares of a stock — the brokerage does not send that order directly to a public stock exchange like the NYSE or Nasdaq. Instead, it routes the order to a wholesale market maker such as Citadel Securities, Virtu Financial, or Jane Street. The market maker executes the trade internally, taking the other side of the customer's order: if the customer wants to buy, the market maker sells from its own inventory; if the customer wants to sell, the market maker buys. The market maker earns the bid-ask spread — the small difference between the price at which it buys and the price at which it sells — on each trade. In return for receiving a steady stream of retail orders, the market maker pays the brokerage a fee. That fee is PFOF.

Suppose a customer buys 1,000 shares of a stock through Robinhood. The national best bid is \$50.00 and the national best ask is \$50.02. Robinhood routes the order to Citadel Securities, which fills the customer's buy order at \$50.01 — a penny better than the public ask (this is called price improvement). Citadel sells to the customer at \$50.01 and can expect to buy the shares back near \$50.00. Citadel earns a spread of \$0.01 per share, or $1{,}000 \times \$0.01 = \$10$ on the trade. Citadel then pays Robinhood a portion of that \$10 — perhaps \$5 to \$8 — as payment for order flow. The customer got a slightly better price than the public quote, the market maker earned a small profit, and the brokerage earned revenue without charging the customer a commission.

Why are market makers willing to pay for retail order flow? The answer lies in [adverse selection](../adverse-selection/). Market makers face the constant risk that their counterparties have better information. A hedge fund that has conducted deep research or has faster data feeds may know something about a stock's near-term direction that the market maker does not. Trading against such an informed counterparty is costly: the market maker buys just before the price falls or sells just before the price rises. Retail customers, by contrast, are generally uninformed. They trade based on personal financial needs, general sentiment, or widely available news rather than proprietary research or speed advantages. For a market maker, trading against a retail customer is one of the safest trades available — the market maker earns the spread with very low risk of adverse selection. Retail order flow is therefore the most profitable kind of flow, and market makers compete to receive it by offering PFOF to brokerages.

PFOF is controversial. Critics argue that it creates a conflict of interest: the brokerage may route orders to whichever market maker pays the most for them, rather than the one that offers the best execution price for the customer. The brokerage earns more revenue by sending orders to the highest-paying market maker, but the customer might have gotten a slightly better fill at a different venue. Proponents argue that PFOF is what enables commission-free trading — the brokerage earns revenue from the trade without charging the customer a fee — and that market makers often provide price improvement, as in the example above, so the customer actually gets a better price than the publicly quoted bid or ask.

PFOF revenue scales with trading volume: more customer trades mean more spreads captured by market makers, which means more PFOF paid to brokerages. This gives retail brokerages a strong incentive to encourage trading activity. It also explains why certain types of trading are more valuable than others. Trades in instruments with wider spreads — such as options, where the bid-ask spread might be several cents, or cryptocurrencies with even wider spreads — generate more PFOF per trade than simple equity trades. This is why brokerages actively promote options trading and, increasingly, cryptocurrency and [prediction market](../prediction-markets/) trading among their retail customers.
