Brokerage cash balance revenue is the interest income a retail brokerage earns on the uninvested cash sitting in customer accounts. It is one of the two main revenue streams for retail brokerages, alongside trade-related revenue such as [payment for order flow](../payment-for-order-flow/). This document builds on [net interest margin](../net-interest-margin/), since the mechanism is the [deposit spread](../net-interest-margin/#the-deposit-spread) by which banks earn money from deposits: the institution takes custody of a customer's idle cash, invests it to earn a market rate of return, and pays the customer a below-market rate on that cash, keeping the difference.

When a customer sells a stock, the proceeds land in the brokerage account as cash. When a customer deposits money to invest but has not yet bought anything, that money also sits as cash. The brokerage does not leave this cash idle. Through a sweep program — an automated system that moves cash into interest-bearing accounts or money market funds at partner banks — the brokerage puts the cash to work and earns interest on it, typically at or near short-term market rates such as the federal funds rate. Meanwhile, the brokerage pays the customer a below-market interest rate on that cash balance. At many discount brokerages, the rate paid to the customer is close to zero.

The spread between what the brokerage earns and what it pays is the cash balance margin. Suppose a customer has \$25,000 in uninvested cash in a brokerage account. The brokerage's sweep program earns 4.5% annually on that cash, or \$1,125 per year. The brokerage pays the customer 0.5%, or \$125 per year. The brokerage keeps the difference,

$$
	\$25{,}000 \times (4.5\% - 0.5\%) = \$1{,}000,
$$

a spread of 400 basis points on the balance. Multiply this across tens of millions of customers holding hundreds of billions of dollars in aggregate cash balances, and the revenue becomes enormous. Charles Schwab, for instance, has historically earned billions of dollars annually from net interest revenue, a significant portion of which comes from customer cash balances.

This revenue stream depends critically on customer inattention. A customer who actively monitors their cash yield would notice that they are earning 0.5% when money market funds or high-yield savings accounts offer 4% or more. Such a customer might move their idle cash into a higher-yielding option — either within the same brokerage (if a better rate is available, such as a money market fund) or by transferring it to a competing institution. When the customer leaves, the brokerage loses both the cash balance and the spread it earned. When many customers do this simultaneously, the brokerage must either raise the rate it pays (compressing its margin) or accept a shrinking deposit base.

The cash balance revenue model therefore creates an inherent tension for brokerages. They benefit from customers who leave cash idle and do not shop around for better rates. This is the same dynamic that makes [net interest margin](../net-interest-margin/) so profitable for banks: the business model is built on customer inertia. Any force that increases customer attention to their cash yields — whether it is comparison websites, financial advisors, or automated optimization tools — threatens this revenue. Conversely, any force that increases customer engagement with trading (generating more [payment for order flow](../payment-for-order-flow/) revenue) benefits the brokerage's other revenue stream. A brokerage's overall profitability depends on the balance between these two effects.
