Market making is the activity of continuously quoting prices at which one is willing to buy (the bid price) and sell (the ask or offer price) a financial instrument, thereby providing liquidity to the market. A market maker is the firm or individual performing this activity. Market makers do not typically take directional bets on whether prices will rise or fall. Instead, they aim to profit from the volume of trading by consistently buying at the bid and selling at the ask, earning the difference between the two prices, known as the bid-ask spread.

Suppose a market maker quotes a bid of \$100.00 and an ask of \$100.05 for shares of Acme Corp. A customer who wants to sell shares immediately will receive \$100.00 per share. A customer who wants to buy shares immediately will pay \$100.05 per share. The \$0.05 difference is the market maker's gross profit per share. If the market maker buys 10,000 shares from sellers and sells 10,000 shares to buyers over the course of a day, it earns $\$0.05 \times 10{,}000 = \$500$ in spread revenue on that stock alone. Across hundreds of stocks and millions of shares per day, this adds up to substantial revenue.

The market maker's central problem is that it does not know in advance whether a given customer is buying or selling, or whether that customer has better information than the market maker does. This uncertainty creates two main risks. The first is [adverse selection](../adverse-selection/): the risk that the market maker trades against a counterparty who knows something about the security's future direction. If the market maker buys at \$100.00 from a hedge fund that knows bad news is about to be released, the market maker will lose money when the stock drops. The second is inventory risk: if more customers are selling than buying (or vice versa), the market maker accumulates a net position. A market maker that ends the day holding 50,000 shares of Acme Corp is exposed to the risk that Acme's price falls overnight. To manage these risks, market makers continuously adjust their quoted prices, hedge their inventory with related instruments, and use sophisticated algorithms to balance their books.

In modern US equity markets, market making is dominated by a handful of large firms including Citadel Securities, Virtu Financial, Jane Street, and Susquehanna International Group. These firms trade electronically at extremely high speeds and handle a substantial fraction of all retail equity trades in the United States. Virtu, for example, has reported being profitable on nearly every trading day over multi-year periods — a testament to the consistency of the spread-earning model when managed at scale with disciplined risk controls.

Market makers operate across many asset classes beyond equities: options, fixed income, foreign exchange, and cryptocurrency. The same fundamental model applies in each market — quote bid and ask prices, earn the spread, manage adverse selection and inventory risk — but the width of the spread varies considerably. Highly liquid large-cap stocks have spreads of a penny or two. Less liquid small-cap stocks may have spreads of five or ten cents. Options, which involve more complexity and uncertainty, typically have spreads of several cents or more. Cryptocurrencies, traded on fragmented and relatively immature exchanges, often have even wider spreads. The less liquid and more uncertain the instrument, the wider the spread must be to compensate the market maker for the risks it bears.

The profitability of market making depends heavily on the type of order flow. Retail order flow is the most profitable because retail customers are generally uninformed (low adverse selection risk), allowing market makers to earn the spread consistently without frequent losses to better-informed counterparties. This is why market makers pay brokerages for retail order flow through [payment for order flow (PFOF)](../payment-for-order-flow/). Institutional order flow, by contrast, carries higher adverse selection risk and commands narrower pricing.
