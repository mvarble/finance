Adverse selection is an economic problem that arises whenever one party to a transaction knows something the other party does not, and exploits that informational advantage. The term originates in insurance markets: people who are most likely to file claims are also the most motivated to buy insurance, so an insurer that prices its policies based on the average risk of the population will attract a pool of customers that is riskier than average. The insurer has been _adversely selected_ — it attracted the wrong customers because it could not tell the good risks from the bad ones.

The problem shows up everywhere in finance, but it is most central to [market making](../market-making/). A market maker continuously quotes prices at which it is willing to buy and sell a security. Every time it does so, it faces the possibility that the person on the other side of the trade knows something it does not. Consider a concrete example: a market maker quotes a bid of $50.00 and an ask of $50.05 on Acme Corp stock. A hedge fund that has spent weeks analyzing Acme's supply chain discovers that a key supplier has gone bankrupt — information the market has not yet priced in. The hedge fund sells 100,000 shares to the market maker at $50.00. When the news breaks and Acme drops to $47, the market maker has lost $3 per share on a trade it would never have made at that price had it known what the hedge fund knew.

This risk is the primary reason market makers charge a bid-ask spread. The spread is not simply a fee for service; it is compensation for the statistical certainty that some fraction of the market maker's counterparties are better informed. A market maker that broke even on informed trades and earned the spread on uninformed trades would be profitable overall. The challenge is that the market maker cannot tell, at the moment of the trade, which counterparties are informed and which are not. It can only set its spread based on the average expected adverse selection across all trades.

This logic explains why market makers care intensely about the _type_ of order flow they receive. Retail customers — individual investors trading from personal accounts — are generally uninformed in the adverse selection sense. They trade based on widely available news, personal financial needs, or general sentiment rather than proprietary research, faster data feeds, or sophisticated quantitative models. A market maker that buys shares from a retail customer who is selling to rebalance a retirement portfolio faces very little risk that the customer knows something about the stock's near-term direction. Retail order flow is therefore the most profitable kind of flow: the market maker earns the spread on nearly every trade with minimal adverse selection losses. This is why market makers pay brokerages a premium — via [payment for order flow](../payment-for-order-flow/) — to receive retail orders.

Institutional order flow carries much higher adverse selection risk. Hedge funds, proprietary trading firms, and quantitative desks often possess better information, faster execution infrastructure, or more sophisticated analytical tools. A market maker trading against such a counterparty faces a meaningful probability of being on the wrong side of an informed bet. For this reason, market makers quote wider spreads to institutional counterparties, or route their orders through different channels with less favorable pricing.

Adverse selection also explains why spreads vary across instruments. A highly liquid large-cap stock like Apple has abundant public information, fast price discovery, and many competing market makers — all of which reduce the information any single counterparty is likely to hold. The adverse selection risk is low, and the spread is a penny or two. A small-cap biotech stock awaiting FDA approval, by contrast, has enormous information asymmetry: someone may know the trial results before they are public. The adverse selection risk is high, and market makers widen their spreads accordingly to compensate.
