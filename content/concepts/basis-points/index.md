title: Basis points

A basis point (abbreviated "bps," pronounced "bips") is a unit of measurement equal to one-hundredth of one percentage point. Ten basis points equal 0.10%, one hundred basis points equal 1.00%, and 250 basis points equal 2.50%.

Basis points exist because percentages are ambiguous when describing changes in rates. If a savings account's interest rate rises from 4% to 5%, it is unclear whether the rate increased by "1 percent" (the absolute difference: 5 − 4 = 1 percentage point) or by "25 percent" (the relative change: 1/4 = 25%). Saying the rate increased by 100 basis points is unambiguous — it always means the absolute change was exactly 0.01 in decimal terms. For this reason, basis points are the standard unit for expressing small differences in interest rates, bond yields, investment returns, and lending spreads throughout finance.

The concept becomes concrete when the dollar value of a single basis point is considered at different scales. On a $10,000 savings account, one basis point is worth $1 per year — an amount too small for any individual to care about. On a bank's $50 billion deposit base, one basis point is worth $5 million per year — an amount that absolutely matters to the bank's bottom line. This scaling property is what makes basis points the natural unit for discussing the margins that financial institutions earn: a bank's [net interest margin](../net-interest-margin/) might be 300 basis points (meaning it earns 3% more on its assets than it pays on its liabilities), and a few basis points of improvement across a large balance sheet can represent tens of millions of dollars.

Basis points also provide a convenient vocabulary for comparing rates across products or institutions. A mortgage broker might offer a rate of 6.75% while a direct lender offers 6.625% — a difference of 12.5 basis points. A money market fund might yield 4.82% while a competing fund yields 4.95% — a difference of 13 basis points. Expressing these differences in basis points makes the comparison immediate and avoids the cognitive overhead of converting between relative and absolute percentage changes.

In discussions of financial technology and automation, basis points often appear as a measure of how much value can be captured through optimization. An individual might not bother switching banks to earn an extra 5 basis points on a savings account, but an automated system managing millions of dollars across many accounts can accumulate meaningful returns from many such small improvements. The phrase "extracting the last basis point" is shorthand for pursuing every available increment of financial improvement, no matter how small each one appears in isolation.
